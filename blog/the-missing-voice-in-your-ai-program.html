The Missing Voice in Your AI Program

Jeff Crump · August 14, 2026

Most AI programs that fail loudest were driven top-down by an executive who read something on LinkedIn and imposed a solution before the technical team could evaluate it.

That’s not a story about bad executives. It’s a story about missing organizational architecture. Every business that adopts AI well has someone technical whose “no” leadership actually listens to. If that person doesn’t exist — or exists but is politically ignored — every AI initiative becomes a downstream problem for engineering to clean up after the check is signed.

The role I’m describing isn’t a CTO title. Plenty of SMBs don’t have a formal CTO, and it works fine. What matters is that somewhere in the organization, there’s a technical voice with the standing to say “not this, not now, not this way” and be heard.

In practice, this looks like:

The AI-curious executive asks about a specific tool. The technical voice runs a two-week evaluation: what problem does it actually solve, what does the current process cost, what does the tool require to deploy, and what’s the exit path if it doesn’t work. Two weeks later, the answer is either “yes, and here’s the plan” or “no, and here’s what to do instead.” Both answers are respected.

Contrast with the failure mode: the executive attends a conference, comes back convinced, signs a $50,000 annual contract, and hands it to the technical team to make work. The technical team can see problems from day one but doesn’t feel authorized to raise them. Twelve months later, the contract renews on autopilot, the tool is used by nobody, and the executive’s next AI initiative arrives with the same shape.

If your organization doesn’t have that voice, or has it but ignores it, that’s a structural problem worth naming before you spend another dollar on AI tooling.

Here’s the diagnostic test: think back to the last three technology decisions of any size. Did a technical person say “wait” at any point? Did their concerns change the decision? If the answer is no on either question, you have a governance gap, and no AI investment will fix it. It has to be fixed first.

This isn’t about hiring a CTO. It’s about deciding whose “no” you’ll listen to before the enthusiasm starts, because the moment you’re in the middle of an AI initiative isn’t the moment you’ll want to hear a technical objection for the first time.
