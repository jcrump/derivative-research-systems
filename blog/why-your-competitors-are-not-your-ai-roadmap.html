Why Your Competitors Are Not Your AI Roadmap

Jeff Crump · September 18, 2026

The origin of an AI initiative predicts its outcome more reliably than the technology chosen.

Programs that begin with “our competitors are doing this” tend to end poorly. Programs that begin with “we have a specific process that costs us N hours per week and AI could reduce it” tend to succeed. The starting language is diagnostic.

I’ve watched this pattern too many times to ignore it. The competitive-anxiety origin produces vague objectives, moving success criteria, and a project that survives despite never delivering because “we can’t be left behind.” The specific-problem origin produces measurable goals, honest reporting, and either a real win or a clean shutdown.

There’s a simple test you can run in the next executive meeting where AI comes up: ask everyone at the table to name three specific processes in the business that AI would improve. Not categories (“customer service” doesn’t count) — specific processes with names and owners.

If the room can produce three, you’re in the specific-problem quadrant. Your job is to prioritize among them.

If the room can produce zero, you’re in the competitive-anxiety quadrant. Your job is not to launch an AI program; your job is to run the inventory exercise until the room can name three.

The distinction matters because the two paths look almost identical in the first ninety days. Both produce budget allocations, tool selections, and executive updates. The difference doesn’t show up until month six, when the specific-problem path has an outcome to point at and the competitive-anxiety path has a series of pivots and a growing list of “we’re still learning.”

Here’s what makes this hard: competitive anxiety is real. Your competitors are doing something with AI, or at least claiming to be. Sitting still while they move feels dangerous. That fear pushes leaders toward action for its own sake, and action for its own sake is where most AI budgets die.

The counterintuitive truth: the businesses winning at AI right now are not the ones moving fastest. They’re the ones with the clearest problem statements. The clarity is what makes their AI investment produce results, and the results are what compound into competitive advantage. Speed alone compounds into technical debt and organizational fatigue.

If your executive team is spending more time talking about what competitors might be doing than about specific processes in your own business, that’s the conversation to change. Start with your own operations. Compete on that, not on the vendor’s roadmap.
